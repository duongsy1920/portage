# AFTERIMAGE

*A design philosophy for surfaces that must be remembered, not merely read.*

---

## The movement

An afterimage is what remains on the retina once the eye has moved away. This
movement takes that residue as its only measure of success. A composition is
finished not when it looks complete but when a viewer who glanced at it for
twenty seconds can still draw its skeleton an hour later. Everything else —
ornament, gradient, cleverness, flourish — is subtracted until only the load
bearing structure remains, and that structure is then built with the patience
of a master instrument maker. The work must look as though someone spent
countless hours aligning it by hand, because that is precisely what separates a
surface that imprints from a surface that decorates.

## Space and form

The plane is treated as a scientific plate: a rectangle under glass, divided by
ruled baselines into fields of unequal weight, each field holding one specimen
and nothing more. Division is never decorative. A hairline exists because two
ideas must not touch; a band of emptiness exists because the eye needs somewhere
to rest before the next observation. Specimens sit in boxes with generous
internal margins, aligned to a single modular grid so ruthlessly that the grid
itself becomes invisible and only its calm remains. Nothing floats. Nothing is
centred by instinct. Every edge answers to another edge, and the painstaking
labour of making those answers exact is the work itself.

## Colour and material

The palette is closed and small — a deep neutral ground, one structural accent,
and two or three semantic hues that never change meaning across the body of
work. Colour is a coding system, not a mood: if a hue names a thing once, it
names that same thing everywhere, forever, and the viewer learns the code
without ever being taught it. Saturation is held low enough that the ground
reads as material rather than as background, and the accents are permitted to
sing only where meaning demands emphasis. This restraint is not poverty. It is
the product of deep expertise, of discarding forty colours to arrive at four.

## Scale and rhythm

Hierarchy is built from ratio, not from shouting. One element is unambiguously
largest and earns that size by carrying the central claim; everything beneath it
steps down in measured intervals so the eye descends a staircase rather than
scanning a field. Repetition is the engine of recall: the same module, the same
corner radius, the same label position, recurring across the plane until the
rhythm itself becomes memorable. Dense accumulation of small, identical marks is
welcome where it rewards sustained viewing, but only when each mark is placed
with the same care as the largest form. Master level execution means the
thousandth detail is as considered as the first.

## Typography

Two voices, never more. A monospaced voice speaks for systems — labels,
identifiers, coordinates, anything a machine would recognise — set tight, in
capitals or lowercase with deliberate letterspacing, treated as drawn form
rather than typed text. A humanist voice speaks for people, in short complete
sentences, set generously, never running longer than a breath. Neither voice
raises itself. Text never wraps awkwardly, never collides, never approaches an
edge without an answer to why. Where a word can be replaced by a mark, it is
replaced. Where a mark would be ambiguous, the word stays and is set perfectly.

## Composition and craft

The finished plate should feel like an artefact from a discipline that does not
quite exist: rigorous, catalogued, numbered, annotated in the margins by someone
who has studied this subject for thirty years. Reference markers, plate numbers
and clinical footnotes are welcome, not as costume but as evidence of a system
that has been used and refined. The paradox at the centre of this movement is
deliberate — analytical visual language applied to something ephemeral, so that
the ephemeral becomes studiable. Above all: nothing overlaps, nothing falls off
the plate, nothing is approximate. The difference between good and museum grade
lives entirely in the final pass, the one where a maker at the top of their
field goes back over finished work and moves things by two pixels because two
pixels was wrong.
